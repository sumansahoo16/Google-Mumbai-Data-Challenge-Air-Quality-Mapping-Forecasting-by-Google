03 / The silence

Missing hours change the story.

A dramatic daily number can come from only a handful of reporting hours. Before comparing dates, first ask whether each monitor was actually present.

Each cell is one station-day. Full color means all 24 hours observed; faint color means fewer; gray means zero. The hour is labelled by its start in India Standard Time.

1,154 of 6,624 possible station-hours are absent between 1 September and 8 November. The data gap is part of the finding, not an invitation to draw a smooth line through it.

05 / The difference

What does one station see that the others miss?

At each hour, subtract the median of the other three stations from one station’s reading. This removes the movement shared at that moment and reveals a local fingerprint.

The line is the median same-hour difference across 1 October–6 November 2026. Only hours when all four stations reported are used: 653 of 888 possible. Positive means above the other three; negative means below. “Night” is 20:00–07:59 IST. Each hour bin contains at least 16 matched observations.

Across matched night hours, Malad West was +7.1 µg/m³ above its three-station peer median; Byculla was -11.9 µg/m³ below. A citywide number can obscure both differences.

This is a comparison among four monitors, not a map of neighborhood exposure. Differences may also reflect monitor behavior. The pattern points to where more local investigation could be useful.

06 / A second lens

Weather helps frame the question.

ClimateVerse lists Open-Meteo’s historical weather API as a challenge resource. We cached modelled weather at the four station coordinates and paired it with days that have at least three reporting monitors.

63 matched days, 1 September–6 November 2026. Weather: median of four modelled daily mean wind speeds, not on-site anemometer readings. PM₂.₅: median of station daily medians with ≥12 hours per station. September gray, October teal, November orange.

The simple rank correlation is -0.23. This is an association across changing seasons; it cannot identify what caused pollution to rise.

07 / The festival

One festival, incomplete evidence.

The 2025 Diwali window shows how quickly pollution can change, but also why a single festival day should not become a permanent forecasting rule.

Malad WestShivaji NagarBycullaBorivali East

2025 Diwali was 20 October. After quality filtering, the 2025 festival date has 57 of 96 possible station-hours; the 2026 date, 8 November, has 0 in this challenge snapshot. Daily medians omit station-days with fewer than 12 readings. Time variation is not proof that fireworks alone caused the change.

On 20 October 2025, available station-day medians were Malad West 115 · Shivaji Nagar 105 · Byculla 100 · Borivali East 94 µg/m³. The 2026 festival’s lack of coverage prevents a like-for-like claim here.

08 / Field notes

Read the signal. Keep the caveats.

A useful air-quality product should show place, time and measurement quality together. This page is a frozen evidence snapshot for conversation and review.

What the page measures

Hourly PM₂.₅ concentration in µg/m³ from the four Zindi challenge stations. The source timestamp is the end of an hour; every analysis shifts it back one hour. Values flagged above 1,000 µg/m³ are removed. Most charts summarize observed hourly values using medians.

What the page does not infer

No neighborhood-wide interpolation, no individual exposure estimate, no causal Diwali effect, and no claim that modelled weather is ground truth. Gaps remain visible. The latest cached target hour is 09 Nov 2026, 06:00 IST.

The finished page needs no network, JavaScript, map tiles or API key. The Python build reads cached CSV snapshots and writes one HTML file with inline SVG charts. Google Fonts are bundled as local WOFF2 files; a Google Colab notebook includes data checks and rebuilds the page from the same cached folder.
